    \textbf{From scatter to distribution function.}\enspace
    The scatter plot in the companion figure displays individual pseudo-observation
    pairs $(u_i, v_i)$---a bivariate \emph{density} view of the data. Point clouds
    like this are natural for revealing clustering, outliers, and the general shape
    of dependence, but they correspond to the joint \emph{density}~$h(u,v)$, not to
    the joint \emph{distribution function}~$H(u,v) = \Pr(U \le u,\, V \le v)$.
    Sklar's theorem, however, operates at the level of \emph{distribution functions}:
    it decomposes the joint CDF, not the joint density. The contour lines overlaid on
    the scatter bridge this gap. Each contour traces out a level set of the copula
    CDF, $\{(u,v) : C(u,v) = c\}$, converting the point-cloud picture into an
    equivalent distributional picture.  In essence, the scatter shows \emph{where mass
    is concentrated} (a density perspective), while the contours show \emph{how much
    mass is accumulated} below any point in the unit square (a CDF perspective).
    Understanding both views simultaneously is key to interpreting what Sklar's
    theorem unlocks: a clean separation of marginal behavior from dependence
    structure, stated entirely in the language of distribution
    functions.$^{\mbox{\footnotesize{1}}}$

    \vspace{6pt}
    \textbf{Sklar's theorem and the copula factorization.}\enspace
    Any bivariate distribution $H(x,y)$ with continuous marginals $F_X$ and $F_Y$
    can be uniquely decomposed as
    %
    \begin{equation}
      H(x,y) \;=\; C\bigl(F_X(x),\, F_Y(y)\bigr),
      \tag{M.4}
    \end{equation}
    %
    where $C\colon [0,1]^2 \to [0,1]$ is the \emph{copula}---a bivariate CDF with
    $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ margins that encodes the full dependence structure
    between $X$ and $Y$ independently of the marginal scales.
    The accompanying figure makes this factorization visually explicit.
    The CDF strips on the bottom and left carry raw scores into the unit square
    ($u = \Fxref(x)$, $v = \Fyref(y)$), while the density/proportion strips
    show how the subgroup's marginals depart from the population baseline.
    What remains in the interior---the bivariate pattern of the
    scatter---is governed entirely by the copula~$C$.$^{\mbox{\footnotesize{2}}}$

    \vspace{6pt}
    \textbf{Interpreting the CDF level curves.}\enspace
    Overlaid on the scatter are level curves of the copula CDF,
    $\{(u,v) : C(u,v) = c\}$ for $c = 0.1, 0.2, \ldots, 0.9$.
    Each curve bounds a region of the unit square such that
    $C(u,v) = c$ means that, under the fitted dependence model, a fraction
    $c$ of the population has \emph{both} a prior pseudo-observation $\leq u$
    and a current pseudo-observation $\leq v$. Equivalently,
    $C(u,v) = \Pr(U \leq u,\; V \leq v)$.
    The spacing and curvature of the contours reflect the strength and shape of
    dependence: under independence ($C = \Pi$, the product copula) these curves
    would be rectangular hyperbolas; with positive dependence they ``bow'' toward
    the main diagonal, concentrating probability mass along
    it.$^{\mbox{\footnotesize{3}}}$

    \vspace{6pt}
    \textbf{Empirical versus parametric copula.}\enspace
    Two sets of contours are displayed: \textit{dashed grey} lines show
    the empirical Bernstein copula estimated non-parametrically from the linked
    data, and \textit{solid colored} lines show the best-fitting parametric copula
    (a \textit{t}-copula with $\mathrm{df} = \copulaDf$).
    The close agreement between the two confirms that the parametric family
    captures the essential dependence features of the data, including symmetric
    tail dependence ($\lambda = \copulaTailLo$) and overall rank
    correlation ($\tau = \copulaTau$, $\rho_s = \copulaRhoSp$).
    Where the two sets of contours diverge---typically in the tails---the
    empirical contours reveal local structure that the parametric model
    smooths over.$^{\mbox{\footnotesize{4}}}$

    \vspace{6pt}
    \textbf{From copula to growth percentiles.}\enspace
    Given the copula $C$, the conditional distribution of current achievement
    given prior achievement follows immediately:
    %
    \begin{equation}
      F_{V \mid U}(v \mid u)
        \;=\; \frac{\partial\, C(u,v)}{\partial\, u},
      \tag{M.5}
    \end{equation}
    %
    which yields a student-level growth percentile for each observed
    pair $(u_i,v_i)$. The CDF level curves in the figure are thus directly
    related to growth percentiles: fixing $u$ and reading off the $v$-values
    at each contour level traces out the conditional quantile function.
    Only \emph{stayers}---students measured at both occasions---contribute paired
    observations to the empirical copula; leavers and entrants appear solely
    in the marginal strips.$^{\mbox{\footnotesize{5}}}$

    \vspace{6pt}
    \textbf{Sensitivity to copula choice.}\enspace
    The primary visual comparison between the empirical and parametric contours
    highlights the robustness (or fragility) of model-based growth percentiles.
    If the contour sets are nearly coincident, as in the present case
    ($\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = \copulaDeltaAIC$ vs.\ the next-best family), the
    parametric copula is an adequate summary and the resulting growth percentiles
    are stable. Visible divergence would signal that percentile estimates near the
    affected quantile regions are sensitive to the copula specification---a key
    diagnostic for growth-model calibration.
