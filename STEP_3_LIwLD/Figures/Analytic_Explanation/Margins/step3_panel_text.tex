    \textbf{The probability-integral transform.}\enspace
    Suppose we observe prior scores $X = (x_1,\dots,x_{n_X})$ and current scores
    $Y = (y_1,\dots,y_{n_Y})$ for a subgroup $S$ (e.g., a TIMSS country or a
    NAEP state). The scores live on assessment-specific metric scales whose
    units carry no intrinsic cross-time meaning. To place both margins on a
    common, metric-free footing we apply fixed \emph{reference} cumulative
    distribution functions (CDFs), computed over the full assessment population,
    to obtain \emph{pseudo-observations} on $[0,1]$:
    %
    \begin{equation}
      u_i \;=\; \Fxref(x_i),
      \qquad
      v_j \;=\; \Fyref(y_j),
      \tag{M.1}
    \end{equation}
    %
    where $\Fxref$ and $\Fyref$ are the prior-grade and current-grade population CDFs,
    respectively. This is the classical \emph{probability-integral transform} (PIT):
    if $\Fxref$ is the true marginal CDF of $X$, then $U = \Fxref(X)$ is uniformly
    distributed on $[0,1]$.

    \vspace{6pt}
    \textbf{Population versus subgroup.}\enspace
    The reference CDFs are defined over the \emph{full population}, not over $S$.
    Consequently, the marginal distribution of $U$ within subgroup~$S$ is
    generally \emph{not} uniform---it reflects $S$'s position relative to the
    population. The marginal density plots in the outer margin strips illustrate
    this: the grey-shaded population density is symmetric and centered, while the
    colored subgroup density is shifted toward lower quantiles, reflecting a
    below-average subgroup. The population-level $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$
    serves as the baseline against which subgroup departures are measured.

    \vspace{6pt}
    \textbf{Stayers, leavers, and entrants.}\enspace
    When prior and current scores come from the \emph{same students} measured
    longitudinally, we observe linked pairs $(x_i, y_i)$---and hence pairs
    $(u_i, v_i)$---that carry information about how achievement at two time
    points co-varies within individuals. These \emph{stayers} appear as
    scatter dots in the center panel and as rug marks on both axes. Formally,
    linked data are draws from a joint law,
    %
    \begin{equation}
      (U_i, V_i) \;\stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\; C\bigl(\Fxref(x), \Fyref(y)\bigr),
      \tag{M.2}
    \end{equation}
    %
    where $C$ is the copula encoding within-subject dependence (Sklar's theorem).
    \emph{Leavers}---students present in the prior round but not the
    current one---appear only as rug marks on the bottom axis. \emph{Entrants}---students
    new to the current round---appear only as rug marks on the left axis. All
    three groups contribute to the marginal distributions, but only stayers
    contribute to the empirical copula.$^{\mbox{\footnotesize{3}}}$

    \vspace{6pt}
    \textbf{Non-linkage and cross-sectional designs.}\enspace
    Cross-sectional assessment programs (NAEP, TIMSS, PIRLS) draw independent
    samples at each occasion. The analyst observes only the marginal
    distributions,
    %
    \begin{equation}
      X_1,\dots,X_{n_X} \;\stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\; F_X,
      \qquad
      Y_1,\dots,Y_{n_Y} \;\stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\; F_Y,
      \tag{M.3}
    \end{equation}
    %
    with no within-subject pairing observed. In terms of the scatter plot,
    this corresponds to having only the rug plots (marginals) with no scatter
    dots (copula). The coupling---the copula $C$ that would connect them---is
    entirely missing and must be supplied by an external model or
    assumption.$^{\mbox{\footnotesize{4}}}$

    \vspace{6pt}
    \textbf{What the marginals preserve and what they discard.}\enspace
    The transform $x \mapsto u = \Fxref(x)$ is monotone and (if $\Fxref$ is
    continuous) invertible, so no ordinal information is lost.$^{\mbox{\footnotesize{1}}}$
    What is discarded is the \emph{metric structure}: the difference
    $x_2 - x_1$ depends on the scale, but the rank ordering
    $u_1 < u_2$ is scale-invariant. This is precisely the property exploited by
    Sklar's theorem, which factors any joint distribution $H(x,y)$ as
    %
    \begin{equation}
      H(x,y) \;=\; C\bigl(F_X(x),\, F_Y(y)\bigr),
      \tag{M.4}
    \end{equation}
    %
    separating the marginal behavior ($F_X$, $F_Y$) from the dependence
    structure ($C$). The CDF strips in the margin make this factorization
    visually explicit: the CDF curves are the ``bridges'' that carry raw scores
    into copula space, and the scatter in the interior reveals the dependence
    structure that remains after the marginals have been removed.
